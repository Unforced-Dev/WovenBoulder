Key Topics

Panel: Adding "Unhoused Status" as a Protected Class

Four panelists each presented for ~10 minutes, followed by commissioner Q&A and discussion.

Ingrid Castro (staff) — Boulder’s Human Rights Ordinance: Existing protected classes: ancestry, color, creed, gender identity, immigration status, mental disability, national origin, physical disability, race, religion, sex, sexual orientation, source of income, age (40+, employment only), pregnancy, custody of a minor child. Claims filed by phone/online/in-person; staff emphasizes mediation and conciliation; quasi-judicial hearings are rare. Ingrid noted she recently launched a monthly educational campaign on existing protected classes.

Kat Ordoñez (ACLU of Colorado, Policy Counsel) — Legal framework: Cities that have already added housing/unhoused status as a protected class: Salem OR, Washington DC, Madison WI, Portland. Misconception addressed: adding it at the city level does not affect constitutional interpretation of protected class doctrine — Boulder would simply choose to protect this class. Existing Boulder HRO already includes situational (non-identity-based) classes like source of income, immigration status, and pregnancy, making housing status legally analogous.

Bill Sweetie (researcher) — Social precarity and comparative law: Introduced the concept of "social precarity": a condition of politically and systemically generated economic and social vulnerability. Each city that has adopted housing protections did so narrowly (1-2 specific areas). Rhode Island has a Homeless Bill of Rights (8 rights: use of public spaces, equal treatment by agencies, equal employment, emergency medical care, right to vote, privacy of records, support animals in public shelters). European Lisbon Declaration: homelessness should be rare, brief, and non-recurring.

Carmen Atilano (former HRC Secretary, 25 years) — Boulder history and enforcement considerations: Five protected classes added since the 1970s ordinance: sexual orientation, genetic characteristics, gender identity (1999-2000), source of income, immigration status (2020). Key questions for the commission: What justification will best withstand opposition? What outcome do you hope to achieve? Is this the best vehicle? Do you have the enforcement arm to back it up? Washington DC considered this for 10 years. Suggested sentence enhancement to Boulder’s existing hate crime ordinance as a potentially simpler vehicle.

Ana Silvia Avendaño (City Equity Policy Adviser) — Racial equity instrument: Presented the city’s six-step racial equity instrument (from GEAR, Government Alliance on Racial Equity, partner since 2018): (1) establish outcomes; (2) collect data; (3) measure benefits and burdens; (4) develop strategies; (5) implement and evaluate; (6) report back publicly. Emphasized the need for a team-based process, flexibility, and getting to root causes.

Commissioner Discussion Lindsay: personal motivation is protection against daily discrimination and the general public disdain toward unhoused people. Christine: not yet sure this is the right vehicle; values it at minimum as a statement of values; needs more study. Speaker: discrimination perpetuates the cycle of homelessness. JH: sees it as hard but important; personal empathy. Carlos: referenced Colorado state bills 1171 (unjust evictions) and 1115 (rent control). Lindsay proposed adding to work plan and drafting a letter to City Council supporting state-level legislation — all commissioners agreed.

Lindsay’s Farewell This was Lindsay’s last meeting as HRC chair. Art Feegal also noted this was his last public comment as a former commissioner. The panel discussion was the culmination of a topic Art introduced in early 2022.
